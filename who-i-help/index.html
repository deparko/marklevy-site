Who I help

Independent professionals and expert-led small businesses.

People whose clients pay for what they know, how they judge, who they know, or what they make. The professions below are examples, not a boundary. If yours isn't here and the description at the bottom fits, the work fits.

Independent consultants and fractional executives

You sell a point of view backed by years of pattern recognition, to people who could tell in ten minutes if it were thin. The week around that judgment is heavy: reading a client's documents before you can say anything useful, writing the memo after, building the deck, keeping five engagements' worth of context straight.

AI belongs in the reading, the first draft, and the memory — summarizing what the client sent at midnight, drafting in your voice, pulling your own past work back into view when a new problem rhymes with an old one. It stops before anything reaches the client. The constraint is that every client's material stays walled off from every other client's. That's a setup question, and it has an answer.

A routine that earns its keep here: the Friday engagement pulse — where each engagement stands, what was promised against what's been delivered, and drafted status notes waiting for your edit.

Financial advisors, CPAs, and other financial professionals

Your work runs on trust and on records: meeting notes, plans, returns, statements, the letter that explains a decision a client will remember for years. You also carry duties about where client information may go, and consumer AI tools were not designed with those duties in mind.

The useful ground is preparation and follow-through. Everything you know about a client, gathered before the meeting. The summary and the next-step letter drafted after it. The annual review that used to take a morning to assemble, assembled. All of it on a business-grade setup you own, with your review before anything is sent. Which configuration satisfies your obligations is the first question we settle, not the last.

A routine that earns its keep here: the Monday pulse on your book — which clients have gone quiet, which reviews are coming due, and drafted check-ins sitting in a folder, waiting for your yes.

Attorneys and other licensed professionals

Documents are the work: reading them, comparing them, marking them up, explaining them. That makes law and the other licensed professions the place where AI helps most, and where getting it wrong costs most — confidentiality is a duty, and the consumer defaults don't honor it.

I don't practice law, and I won't pretend to. What I do is set the tool up so privileged material stays where it should, write down your own positions and standards so the first pass reflects them, and build a review step you can't skip. A redline that arrives with your usual positions already applied, waiting for your judgment, is a good afternoon. A draft that went out without your judgment on it is the thing we're preventing.

A routine that earns its keep here: the month-end matter packet — matters gone quiet, unbilled time, and drafted follow-ups, assembled and waiting for your review before anything moves.

Creative and entertainment professionals

Writers, producers, designers, composers, agents, managers, and the people who represent them. Your value is taste and relationships, and AI-made work in your field draws the loudest backlash of any — so the line is bright: I never build anything your audience or your clients see.

Behind the scenes, there's a lot of room. Deal memos and agreements summarized before the call. The research on a buyer or a market done in an hour instead of a day. Your correspondence drafted in your own cadence. Years of notes and contacts turned into something you can search. The creative work stays yours. The paperwork around it gets lighter.

A routine that earns its keep here: the pre-call packet — everything you know about the person and the project on one page, with the open questions and the last three things promised, before you pick up the phone.

Coaches, recruiters, and relationship-driven practices

Executive coaches, recruiters and search consultants, real-estate professionals — businesses that run on conversations and on remembering everything about a lot of people. The waste is in the aftermath: the notes, the follow-up, the profile you promised, the market summary a client asked for on the way out the door.

AI can turn a rough note into a clean summary and a drafted next step, keep a searchable memory of every relationship, and prepare you for the next conversation with everything you already know. The sensitive part is the people. What gets recorded, what gets stored, where it lives, and who can see it are decided before anything is switched on.

A routine that earns its keep here: the after-the-conversation routine — your rough notes become a clean summary and a drafted follow-up, and nothing goes out until you've read both.

Boutique expert-led firms

The firm of three to thirty, built around a founder's name and a founder's standards. You carry the liability of a company ten times your size and none of its departments.

The opportunity is that your standards can be written down once and applied everywhere: how a proposal is structured, how client communication sounds, the checks a deliverable goes through before it leaves. I work with the principal to get those standards out of their head and into working tools the whole team uses, on accounts the firm owns, with the principal's sign-off still in the loop. The playbook becomes an asset the firm holds, and it stays when people leave.

A routine that earns its keep here: the weekly promised-versus-delivered check — every engagement's commitments against what actually shipped, on the principal's desk Monday morning.

What these businesses have in common

  • Established and successful. You're past figuring out whether the business works.
  • Clients pay for your expertise, your judgment, your relationships, or the work you create — rather than for throughput or inventory.
  • You're the one who decides. No committee, no procurement, no IT department to hand this to.
  • You care about privacy, accuracy, reputation, and control, and you'd rather hear "skip it" than buy something you don't need.
  • You've used ChatGPT. You're not against it. You want to know where it fits and where it should stay out.

Who this isn't for

Businesses whose main challenges are operational rather than knowledge-based — a restaurant, a retail store, a trade with trucks to route — need something different from what I do, and I'd rather say so than take the engagement.

Also not for: firms with an IT department. Anyone who wants to fully automate client work with no human review. Anyone shopping for the cheapest prompt list.
